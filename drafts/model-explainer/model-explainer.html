Part A · The big picture

1. The problem and the engine at a glance

In the Mekong Delta (southern Vietnam) seawater moves up the rivers in the dry season (roughly January–May). Canal water becomes too salty for rice. Farmers ask: should I keep growing 3 rice crops a year, or change plan? The engine compares 5 plans at 5 places (pins). A plan is a list of 12 codes, one per month:

PlanJFMAMJJASOND

2 rice cropsR1R1-R2R2R2R2---R1R1

Rice + upland cropUU-R1R1R1R1R2R2R2-U
Salt-tolerant riceR1---R2R2R2R2-R1R1R1

The engine is a deterministic function: no randomness, no fitted parameters. Its output for every plan at every pin is four scores in $[1,5]$ with one decimal place, plus a confidence level:

Figure 1. Data flow through the engine.
The single most important idea: a reference point Almost every score compares a plan with 3 rice crops at the same pin. 3 rice crops always score Water 1.0 and Income 3.0 by construction. A score therefore measures "better or worse than today's habit here", not an absolute quality.

3. Toolkit

3.1 Functions and composition

Each score is a composite function: data $\to$ a ratio $\to$ a raw score $\to$ clamp. For example $\text{Water}=c\circ \ell\circ s$ where $s$ is "fraction of water saved", $\ell(s)=1+4s$ and $c$ is the clamp below.

9709 P1 1.2 FunctionsComposite $fg(x)$, domain and range, one-one functions and inverses. Each score map is one-one on the part of the domain where it is not clamped, so it has an inverse there (used in Exercise 6).

3.2 Clamp and cap, written with modulus

$$\operatorname{clamp}(x,a,b)=\min\big(\max(x,a),b\big),\qquad \max(p,q)=\tfrac12\big(p+q+|p-q|\big),\qquad \min(p,q)=\tfrac12\big(p+q-|p-q|\big).$$

How you get the max formula. If $p\ge q$ then $|p-q|=p-q$ and $\tfrac12(p+q+p-q)=p$. If $p<q$ then $|p-q|=q-p$ and the expression is $q$. So clamp is built entirely from the modulus function. A cap $\min(x,1)$ means "more than 1 earns nothing extra".

9709 P3 3.1 AlgebraThe modulus function and graphs of $y=|f(x)|$. The graph of a clamp is a straight line with its ends flattened: two "corners", like a modulus graph.

3.3 Sigma notation, median, logarithms

  • $\sum_{m=1}^{12} N_m$ adds the 12 monthly values (9231 FP1 1.3).
  • Median: the middle of a sorted list; for even length, the mean of the two middle values (9709 S1 5.1).
  • $\log_2 x=\dfrac{\ln x}{\ln 2}$ (change of base, 9709 P3 3.2); $\log_2 2=1$, $\log_2 1=0$, $\log_2\tfrac12=-1$.

Part B · From weather to water

4. Climate averages and El Niño

NASA data gives daily weather from 1 Dec 2000 to 30 Nov 2024: 24 "seasons" running December–November. For calendar month $m$ and season $y$ let $x_{m,y}$ be the monthly total. The engine uses the sample mean over the seasons kept:

$$\bar x_m=\frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{y\in Y}x_{m,y}.$$

El Niño is a warming of the Pacific that brings drier, saltier dry seasons to the Delta. A season counts as El Niño when NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index for December–February is at least 0.5. Between 2000 and 2024 that happened in 9 of the 24 seasons (starting 2002, 2004, 2006, 2009, 2014, 2015, 2018, 2019, 2023). The typical year uses $Y$ = the other 15 seasons; the El Niño year uses those 9.

9709 S2 6.4 Sampling and estimation; S1 5.3 conditional probability$\bar x_m$ is an unbiased estimate of the population mean of month $m$. Splitting by El Niño gives two conditional means: "mean rain in March, given an El Niño season". The relative frequency $9/24=0.375$ estimates P(a season is El Niño).

Each month's mean is computed from daily values: rain $P$, evaporation $ET_0$ (Sections 5–6) and the count of days with top temperature $\ge 35^\circ$C.

5. Sunshine at the top of the atmosphere, $R_a$

Evaporation is driven by the Sun, so the first physical quantity is $R_a$: the solar energy reaching 1 m² of horizontal ground above the atmosphere in a day (MJ m−2 day−1). The code (ra, FAO-56 eq. 21) is

$$R_a=\frac{24\times60}{\pi}\,G_{sc}\,d_r\Big(\omega_s\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin\omega_s\Big),\qquad G_{sc}=0.082\ \text{MJ m}^{-2}\text{min}^{-1}.$$

where $\varphi$ = latitude (radians), $\delta$ = the Sun's declination, $d_r$ = Earth–Sun distance factor, $\omega_s$ = sunset hour angle. Here is how every piece is obtained.

5.1 Seasonal inputs are sinusoidal models

$$\delta=0.409\sin\!\Big(\frac{2\pi J}{365}-1.39\Big),\qquad d_r=1+0.033\cos\!\Big(\frac{2\pi J}{365}\Big),\qquad J=\text{day of the year}.$$

$\delta$ is a sine curve with period 365 days and amplitude $0.409$ rad $=23.4^\circ$, the tilt of the Earth's axis. The phase shift $1.39$ rad puts $\delta=0$ near 21 March (equinox). $d_r$ varies by ±3.3% because the orbit is slightly elliptical: largest in early January when the Earth is closest to the Sun.

9709 P1 1.4 Circular measure; P1 1.5 TrigonometryGraphs of $y=a\sin(bx+c)$: amplitude $a$, period $2\pi/b$, phase shift. Here $b=2\pi/365$, so the period is 365.

5.2 The angle of the Sun, from a scalar product

Put the centre of the Earth at the origin with the $z$-axis along the Earth's axis. A point at latitude $\varphi$ whose "hour angle" from noon is $\omega$ has upward unit normal, and the Sun has unit direction,

$$\mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi\cos\omega\\ \cos\varphi\sin\omega\\ \sin\varphi\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf s=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta\\0\\ \sin\delta\end{pmatrix}.$$

The angle $\theta$ between the vertical and the Sun satisfies $\cos\theta=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s$:

$$\cos\theta=\cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos\omega+\sin\varphi\sin\delta.$$

Energy on a horizontal surface is proportional to $\cos\theta$ (a beam hitting at a slant spreads over more area).

9709 P3 3.7 Vectors; 9231 FP1 1.6 Vectors$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta$. Both vectors have length 1 (check: $\cos^2\varphi(\cos^2\omega+\sin^2\omega)+\sin^2\varphi=1$).

5.3 Sunset: solving a trigonometric equation

The Sun sets when it reaches the horizon, $\cos\theta=0$:

$$\cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos\omega_s+\sin\varphi\sin\delta=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \cos\omega_s=-\tan\varphi\tan\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ \omega_s=\cos^{-1}(-\tan\varphi\tan\delta).$$

Day length in hours is $\dfrac{24\,\omega_s}{\pi}$, since $\omega$ turns through $2\pi$ in 24 hours and daylight lasts from $-\omega_s$ to $\omega_s$.

5.4 Integrating over the day

The hour angle turns $2\pi$ radians in $1440$ minutes, so $dt=\dfrac{1440}{2\pi}\,d\omega$. The day's energy is the area under the power curve from sunrise to sunset:

$$R_a=\int G_{sc}d_r\cos\theta\,dt=\frac{1440}{2\pi}G_{sc}d_r\int_{-\omega_s}^{\omega_s}\big(\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos\omega\big)\,d\omega$$ $$=\frac{1440}{2\pi}G_{sc}d_r\Big[\omega\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin\omega\Big]_{-\omega_s}^{\omega_s}=\frac{1440}{\pi}G_{sc}d_r\big(\omega_s\sin\varphi\sin\delta+\cos\varphi\cos\delta\sin\omega_s\big).$$

The factor 2 from the symmetric limits turns $\frac{1440}{2\pi}$ into $\frac{24\times 60}{\pi}$: exactly the formula in the code.

9709 P1 1.8 and P3 3.5 Integration$\int\cos\omega\,d\omega=\sin\omega$; definite integrals as areas; changing variable from time to angle. $\sin$ is odd, so $\sin\omega_s-\sin(-\omega_s)=2\sin\omega_s$.

6. Evaporation: the Penman–Monteith equation

$ET_0$ is the water (mm per day) a well-watered grass field loses to the air. FAO-56 equation 6, used for every day:

$$ET_0=\frac{0.408\,\Delta\,R_n+\gamma\,\dfrac{900}{T+273}\,u_2\,(e_s-e_a)}{\Delta+\gamma\,(1+0.34\,u_2)}$$

Top line: an energy term (sunshine kept by the field, $R_n$) plus an air term (wind $u_2$ × how dry the air is, $e_s-e_a$). Below are the pieces.

6.1 Saturation vapour pressure: an exponential model

$$e^\circ(T)=0.6108\exp\!\Big(\frac{17.27\,T}{T+237.3}\Big)\ \text{kPa}$$

This is the most water vapour air at $T$ °C can hold. It grows roughly exponentially: each extra degree lets air hold about 6% more water.

6.2 The gradient $\Delta$: quotient rule and chain rule

Let $g(T)=\dfrac{17.27T}{T+237.3}$. Quotient rule: $g'(T)=\dfrac{17.27(T+237.3)-17.27T}{(T+237.3)^2}=\dfrac{4098.2}{(T+237.3)^2}$. Chain rule:

$$\Delta=\frac{d}{dT}\Big(0.6108\,e^{g(T)}\Big)=0.6108\,e^{g(T)}g'(T)=\frac{4098\,e^\circ(T)}{(T+237.3)^2}.$$
9709 P3 3.4 Differentiation; P3 3.2 exponentials$\frac{d}{dx}e^{f(x)}=f'(x)e^{f(x)}$; quotient rule. This is the exact expression FAO prints and the code uses.

6.3 Why average $e^\circ$ at $T_{\max}$ and $T_{\min}$, not $e^\circ$ at the mean?

The code uses $e_s=\tfrac12\big(e^\circ(T_{\max})+e^\circ(T_{\min})\big)$. Because $e^\circ$ is convex ($\frac{d^2e^\circ}{dT^2}>0$, the curve bends upward), the chord lies above the curve, so

$$\tfrac12\big(e^\circ(T_{\max})+e^\circ(T_{\min})\big)\ \ge\ e^\circ\!\Big(\tfrac{T_{\max}+T_{\min}}{2}\Big).$$

On 15 March 2024: $\tfrac12(4.5699+3.3258)=3.9479$ kPa, but $e^\circ(28.56)=3.9049$ kPa. Using the mean temperature would understate how much water the air can take (this inequality is called Jensen's inequality).

9709 P1 1.7 Second derivativeThe sign of $f''$ tells you which way a curve bends. A positive second derivative means a chord lies above the curve.

6.5 Why 0.408? Converting energy to millimetres

Evaporating 1 kg of water needs about 2.45 MJ (latent heat). 1 mm of water over 1 m² is $10^{-3}\,\text{m}^3=1$ kg. So 1 MJ m−2 of energy evaporates $\frac{1}{2.45}=0.408$ mm.

7. From days to months: sums and integrals

The month's evaporation is the sum of its daily values: $E_m=\sum_{\text{days }d\in m}ET_0(d)$. Each day is a rectangle of width 1 day and height $ET_0(d)$, so $E_m$ approximates the area $\int ET_0(t)\,dt$ under a smooth evaporation curve.

9231 FP2 2.4 Integration; 9709 P1 1.8Using rectangles to bound and approximate an integral. With 30 thin rectangles a month, the error is small.

7.1 The water balance as a differential equation

Let $S(t)$ be the water stored in a flooded rice field (mm). It changes by inflow minus outflow:

$$\frac{dS}{dt}=\underbrace{r(t)+i(t)}_{\text{rain, irrigation}}-\underbrace{k\,e(t)}_{\text{crop use}}-\underbrace{q}_{\text{seepage}}.$$

A farmer keeps the water level steady, so $\frac{dS}{dt}=0$. Integrating over a month and solving for irrigation $I=\int i\,dt$:

$$I=K_cE_m+\text{PERC}_m-P_{\text{eff}}(P_m).$$

That is the heart of the canal-water formula in Section 8; the one-off terms SAT and WL add the water needed to fill the store at the start.

9709 P3 3.8 Differential equationsForming a first-order equation from "rate in − rate out". Here we only need the steady state, $dS/dt=0$.

8.1 Crop factor: a piecewise function

$$K_c(j)=\begin{cases}1.05 & j=1\\ 1.20 & 1\lt j\lt L\\ 0.75 & j=L\end{cases}\qquad (j=\text{month of the crop's life},\ L=\text{its length})$$

8.2 Effective rain: continuity at the join

$$P_{\text{eff}}(P)=\begin{cases}0.8P-25 & P>75\\ \max(0.6P-10,0) & P\le75\end{cases}$$

At $P=75$ both rules give 35, so the function is continuous (no jump). The gradient rises from 0.6 to 0.8: in wet months more of each extra millimetre soaks in. And $0.6P-10<0$ for $P<\frac{50}{3}\approx16.7$, hence the inner max.

8.4 Crop water as a scalar product

Write the crop factors and evaporations as 12-component vectors $\mathbf K$ and $\mathbf E$. The crop's own water use for the year is

$$\mathbf K\cdot\mathbf E=\sum_{m=1}^{12}K_mE_m=1.20(122)+0.75(132)+0+\dots+1.20(110)=1386\ \text{mm}\quad\text{(3 rice crops).}$$

The other 539 mm of $W$ is soaking, flooding and seepage minus usable rain, with the $\max$ applied month by month.

9709 P3 3.7 Vectors; 9231 FP1 1.4 MatricesA scalar product is a sum of products of matching components. The idea works in 12 dimensions just as in 3.

8.5 How big can rounding errors be?

Each $N_m$ is rounded to a whole mm before summing. If $e_m$ is the rounding error, $|e_m|\le\tfrac12$. Claim: $\big|\sum_{m=1}^{n}e_m\big|\le\tfrac n2$. Proof by induction. True for $n=1$. If true for $n=k$, then $\big|\sum_{1}^{k+1}e_m\big|\le\big|\sum_1^ke_m\big|+|e_{k+1}|\le\tfrac k2+\tfrac12=\tfrac{k+1}{2}$ (triangle inequality). So the yearly total is within 6 mm of the unrounded value: under 0.4% of 1925.

Part C · The four scores

9. Water score

$$s=\max\!\Big(1-\frac{W}{W_3},0\Big)\ \ \text{(fraction saved)},\qquad \boxed{\text{Water}=\operatorname{clamp}(1+4s,1,5)}$$

How you get it. We want 1 when nothing is saved and 5 when all is saved, changing steadily: the straight line through $(0,1)$ and $(1,5)$. Gradient $\frac{5-1}{1-0}=4$, intercept 1, so $1+4s$.

10. Weather & salt score

10.1 From a risk to a part score

Each risk $r\in[0,1]$ becomes a part score via the line through $(0,5)$ and $(1,1)$: $\text{part}=5-4r=1+4(1-r)$.

10.2 The three risks

Let $\mathcal S$ be the pin's salty months (from station reports; never measured by satellite), $n=|\mathcal S|$.

$$r_{\text{salt}}=\frac1n\sum_{m\in\mathcal S}w_m\times\min\!\Big(\frac{c}{15},1\Big),\quad w_m=\begin{cases}1&\text{rice or upland}\\0.5&\text{salt-tolerant rice}\\0&\text{shrimp or rest}\end{cases}$$ $$r_{\text{heat}}=\min\!\Big(\frac{H}{30},1\Big),\qquad r_{\text{canal}}=\min\!\Big(\frac{C}{C_3},1\Big)$$
  • $c$: the station's average highest reading per dry season (‰). From 15‰ the severity is full.
  • $H$: hot days ($\ge35^\circ$C) in months with rice or upland crops; rice flowers fail above 35 °C (IRRI). 30 days = full risk (team choice).
  • $C=\sum_{m\in\mathcal S}N_m$; $C_3$ = the same for 3 rice crops.
Why multiply in $r_{\text{salt}}$?Damage needs a crop in the field and salty water. A product is 0 if either factor is 0, like $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ for independent events (9709 S1 5.3). The factors here are shares, not true probabilities, so this is an analogy, not a probability model.

10.3 The weighted mean, as an expectation

$$\boxed{\text{Weather \& salt}=0.6\,p_{\text{salt}}+0.2\,p_{\text{heat}}+0.2\,p_{\text{canal}}}$$

Proof it stays in $[1,5]$. Weights are positive with sum 1. If $1\le p_i\le5$, then $\sum w_ip_i\ge\sum w_i=1$ and $\sum w_ip_i\le5\sum w_i=5$. ∎

As an expectation. Let $X$ be the part score of a randomly chosen "risk", chosen with probabilities $0.6,0.2,0.2$. Then the score is $\mathrm E(X)=\sum x\,P(X=x)$ (9709 S1 5.4). An expectation always lies between the smallest and largest value.

10.4 Matrix form for all five plans

Put each plan's parts in a row of a $5\times3$ matrix $\mathbf A$ and the weights in a column $\mathbf w$. Then all five scores are one matrix product (Vĩnh Châu, typical year):

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1.8&5&1\\1.8&5&1\\5&5&5\\1.8&5&1.906\\4.2&5&2.948\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf A\ (\text{salt, heat, canal})}\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0.2\\0.2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2.28\\2.28\\5.00\\2.46\\4.11\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}2.3\\2.3\\5.0\\2.5\\4.1\end{pmatrix}\begin{matrix}\text{3 rice}\\ \text{2 rice}\\ \text{rice–shrimp}\\ \text{rice + upland}\\ \text{salt-tolerant}\end{matrix}$$

10.6 How sensitive is it to the weights?

Let the salt weight be $\lambda$ and split the rest equally: $F(\lambda)=\lambda p_{\text{salt}}+\frac{1-\lambda}{2}(p_{\text{heat}}+p_{\text{canal}})$. Then

$$F'(\lambda)=p_{\text{salt}}-\tfrac12(p_{\text{heat}}+p_{\text{canal}}).$$

For 3 rice crops $F'=1.8-3=-1.2$: raising the salt weight from 0.6 to 0.7 lowers the score by $0.12$. The ranking of the plans is what matters, and a team-chosen weight can shift it, which is why the app lowers confidence when team estimates are used.

12. Income score

12.1 Yearly profit from medians

$$P=\sum_{\text{crops }k}\operatorname{median}(\text{figures}_k)\times(1-L_k)$$

Profit figures (million đồng per hectare) come from published studies, 2020–2025, each checked against its source. The median is robust: for summer–autumn rice $\{17, 25.4, 30\}$ the median is 25.4; one wrong figure of 90 would move the mean from 24.1 to 40.6 but the median only to 27.7.

$L_k$ is the expected share of the crop lost. If a fraction $L$ is lost on average, the expected profit is $\mathrm E(\text{profit})=m(1-L)$ (9709 S1 5.4). In the El Niño view rice grown in salty months has $L=0.1$; shrimp has $L=0.092$ in every year.

12.2 Deriving the score: a functional equation

We want a function $f$ of the ratio $x=P/P_3$ with: $f(1)=3$; $f(2)=5$; and multiplying profit by the same factor always adds the same amount: $f(\lambda x)-f(x)$ depends only on $\lambda$. Functions with that property are $f(x)=A+B\log_2x$, because $f(\lambda x)-f(x)=B\log_2\lambda$. Then $f(1)=3\Rightarrow A=3$; $f(2)=5\Rightarrow B=2$:

$$\boxed{\text{Income}=\operatorname{clamp}\Big(3+2\log_2\frac{P}{P_3},1,5\Big)}\quad(\text{and }1.0\text{ if }P\le0)$$
  • Symmetry: $f(\frac1x)=3-2\log_2x=6-f(x)$. Half the profit is as far below 3 as double is above.
  • Transformations: $y=2\log_2x+3$ is $y=\log_2x$ stretched by factor 2 parallel to the $y$-axis, then translated by $\binom{0}{3}$ (9709 P1 1.2).
  • Inverse: $x=2^{(\text{Income}-3)/2}$. Income 4 needs $x=\sqrt2\approx1.41$.
Figure 3. Income score, clamped at 1 (for $x\le\frac12$) and 5 (for $x\ge2$).

12.3 Rate of change and a Maclaurin approximation

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(3+2\log_2x\big)=\dfrac{2}{x\ln2}$. At $x=\tfrac12$ the gradient is 5.77; at $x=1$, 2.89; at $x=2$, 1.44. A plan earning less than today gains score quickly as profit rises; above today the gains slow down.

For a small rise $u$ in profit ($x\to x(1+u)$) the score rises by $2\log_2(1+u)=\frac{2}{\ln2}\ln(1+u)\approx\frac{2}{\ln 2}\big(u-\frac{u^2}{2}\big)$. With $u=0.1$: approximation $0.274$; exact $2\log_21.1=0.275$. So each 1% more profit adds about 0.029 points.

9709 P3 3.4; 9231 FP2 2.3 Maclaurin series$\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x$; $\ln(1+u)=u-\frac{u^2}{2}+\frac{u^3}{3}-\dots$ for $|u|<1$.

13. Confidence

An integer counter $q$: 2 = High, 1 = Medium, 0 = Low.

  1. Start $q=2$.
  2. Subtract 1 for each: (i) weak income evidence: a crop has fewer than 2 good (non-fallback) figures, or $\max/\min>2$, or no figure; (ii) no satellite greenness data; (iii) salinity station with fewer than 5 recent seasons or no reading after 2022; (iv) shrimp planned where there is no salty water; (v) any team estimate used (all together count once).
  3. Add 1 if satellite images show fields bare in Feb–Apr and the plan is upland or salt-tolerant.
  4. $q\leftarrow\operatorname{clamp}(q,0,u)$ with $u=1$ if (i) happened, else $u=2$.

Examples. 3 rice crops: autumn–winter rice has only 1 good figure (27.9; the rest are fallbacks), and the station's last reading is 2022: $q=2-1-1=0$, Low. 2 rice crops: only the station rule, $q=1$, Medium.

14. Sowing window

The app tells farmers when they can sow so the crop avoids all salty months. A crop of $D$ days started on the 1st of month $s$ occupies the months $s,s+1,\dots$ until the cumulative days reach $D$. Months wrap round with modular arithmetic: month after $m$ is $(m \bmod 12)+1$.

15. Counting crops from space (NDVI)

NDVI is a satellite "greenness" number: about 0 for bare soil or water, about 0.8 for a dense green crop (NASA MODIS, every 16 days, 250 m pixels).

  1. Smoothing with a running median. Each value is replaced by the median of itself and its two neighbours, removing single cloudy dips: $\tilde v_i=\operatorname{median}(v_{i-1},v_i,v_{i+1})$.
  2. Peaks = local maxima. Point $i$ is a crop peak if $\tilde v_i\ge0.5$, $\tilde v_i\ge\tilde v_{i-1}$ and $\tilde v_i>\tilde v_{i+1}$: the discrete version of "$f'(x)=0$ and $f''(x)<0$". Two peaks closer than 60 days count once (keep the higher).
  3. Crops per year $=\dfrac{\text{number of peaks}}{\text{years covered}}$. Vĩnh Châu: 3.0, matching 3 rice crops.
  4. Bare months: month $m$ is "usually bare" if its mean NDVI is below 0.3 in more than half the years (a majority rule).
9709 P1 1.7 Stationary points; S1 5.1A maximum is where the gradient changes from positive to negative; here "gradient" is the difference between neighbouring points. The running median is robust to outliers in the same way as in Section 12.1.

16. Checking rain data: correlation

Two NASA rain sources are compared month by month over 288 months using Pearson's product-moment correlation coefficient:

$$r=\frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}},\qquad S_{xy}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y),\ S_{xx}=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2.$$

At Vĩnh Châu $r=0.93$: the two sources rise and fall together, but GPM's yearly total is 14% higher (1993 vs 1746 mm). $r$ measures agreement in pattern, not in size. (Correlation is not on the current 9709/9231 syllabuses, but uses only the summation notation of S1 5.1.)

17. Salinity units and a sign test

17.1 Units: direct proportion

Salinity is in parts per thousand (‰ = g/L). FAO uses electrical conductivity: TDS (mg/L) $\approx640\times$EC (dS/m). So $1$‰ $=1000$ mg/L $\approx\frac{1000}{640}=1.56$ dS/m. Maize starts losing yield at 1.1 dS/m $\approx0.7$‰; rice at 3.0 dS/m $\approx1.9$‰. Levels used for the area badge: low below 4‰, medium 4–15‰, high from 15‰.

17.2 Are El Niño seasons saltier? A sign test

At all 8 salinity stations, the average dry-season peak over 2001–2024 was higher in El Niño seasons than in other seasons. Is that just chance?

$H_0$: El Niño makes no difference, so each station is equally likely to be higher or lower. $H_1$: El Niño seasons are saltier (one-tailed). Under $H_0$, $X$ = number of stations higher in El Niño $\sim B(8,0.5)$.

$$P(X\ge8)=\Big(\tfrac12\Big)^8=\tfrac{1}{256}=0.0039<0.05.$$

Reject $H_0$ at the 5% level: strong evidence that El Niño seasons are saltier. Caution: the stations are close together and share the same weather, so they are not truly independent; the real evidence is weaker than $p=0.0039$ suggests.

9231 FS 4.4 Non-parametric tests; 9709 S1 5.4 Binomial; S2 6.5 Hypothesis testsThe sign test counts "+" signs and uses $B(n,\frac12)$; independence is one of its assumptions.

19. Modelling: assumptions, strengths, limits

Assumptions and limitsStrengths
  • Monthly means hide bad single years (only the El Niño view partly covers this).
  • Constants without a measured source: weights 0.6/0.2/0.2, $w=0.5$ for tolerant rice, $\beta=0.5$, 30 hot days. They lower confidence.
  • Clamping hides differences at the ends of the scale.

  12. Suppose only 7 of the 8 stations were saltier in El Niño years. Carry out the sign test at the 5% level. (FS 4.4)

Solutions

  1. $0.8(100)-25=55$. $\max(0.6(15)-10,0)=\max(-1,0)=0$. Both pieces give 35 at $P=75$, so the left and right limits equal $P_{\text{eff}}(75)=35$: continuous.
  2. If $p\le q$: $|p-q|=q-p$, giving $\frac12(2p)=p=\min$. If $p>q$: $|p-q|=p-q$, giving $q$. ∎
  3. $\tan\varphi=0.16493$, $\tan0.409=0.4337$; $\cos\omega_s=-0.07153$, $\omega_s=1.6424$ rad; day length $=24(1.6424)/\pi=12.55$ h (longer than at the equinox, 11.95 h).
  4. $[\sin\omega]_{-\omega_s}^{\omega_s}=\sin\omega_s-\sin(-\omega_s)=2\sin\omega_s$. $\cos$ is even, so the graph is symmetric about $\omega=0$ and the two halves have equal area.
  5. $\frac{de^\circ}{dT}=e^\circ g'$ with $g'=\frac{4098.2}{(T+237.3)^2}$. Product rule: $\frac{d^2e^\circ}{dT^2}=e^\circ(g')^2+e^\circ g''$, with $g''=\frac{-8196.4}{(T+237.3)^3}$. So $\frac{d^2e^\circ}{dT^2}=e^\circ\cdot\frac{4098.2}{(T+237.3)^3}\Big(\frac{4098.2}{T+237.3}-2\Big)$. Here $e^\circ>0$ and $T+237.3>0$, and the bracket is positive when $T+237.3<2049.1$, i.e. $T<1811.8$. So it is positive for every temperature that occurs in weather.
  6. $1+4(0.3)=2.2$. $3.5=1+4s\Rightarrow s=0.625$ (62.5% saved).
  7. $3+2\log_2x=2\Rightarrow\log_2x=-\frac12\Rightarrow x=2^{-1/2}=0.707$.
  8. In $[\frac12,2]$ no clamping: $(3+2\log_2x)+(3-2\log_2x)=6$. ∎
  9. $0.9+1.25+0.25=2.4$; $2.4$; $5.0$; $0.9+1.25+0.477=2.63\to2.6$; $2.1+1.25+0.737=4.09\to4.1$. Order unchanged: rice–shrimp, salt-tolerant, rice + upland, then the two rice plans.
  10. Need $P_{\text{eff}}=270.4$; since this is over 35, use $0.8P-25=270.4\Rightarrow P=369.25$ mm, about 14 times January's actual mean rain (27 mm).
  11. $u=0.2$: $\frac{2}{\ln2}(0.2-0.02)=0.519$; exact $2\log_21.2=0.526$. Close; the next term $+\frac{u^3}{3}$ would close the gap.
  12. $P(X\ge7)=\frac{\binom87+\binom88}{256}=\frac{9}{256}=0.0352<0.05$: still reject $H_0$.
